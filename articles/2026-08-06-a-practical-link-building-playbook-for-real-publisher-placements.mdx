## Step 1: Define the campaign before requesting links

Start with the business goal, not the tool. A campaign designed to promote a local service page will need different placements from one supporting a national ecommerce category or a software comparison page.

Write down five details before you begin:

1. **The target URL:** Choose the page that should receive visibility. This could be a product page, service page, category page, resource guide, or homepage.
2. **The audience:** Describe who should find the page and what problem they are trying to solve.
3. **The geographic focus:** Note whether the campaign is local, national, or international.
4. **The topical neighborhood:** List subjects that should be relevant to the target page.
5. **The desired number of placements:** Set a realistic quantity based on budget, timeline, and the strength of the page being promoted.

For example, a freelance SEO might promote a guide about commercial cleaning contracts. The brief could specify business owners and office managers as the audience, a national English-speaking market, and related topics such as facility management, workplace hygiene, and vendor selection.

A precise brief gives publishers enough context to create a natural placement. It also helps you evaluate whether the resulting page supports the campaign instead of merely containing a link.

## Step 2: Choose the right target page and anchor strategy

The page receiving a link should be genuinely useful to the reader who encounters it. Sending every placement to a homepage may be convenient, but it often produces a less relevant experience. A strong campaign typically distributes attention among pages that deserve discovery.

Review the target page for the following qualities:

- It answers a clear question or supports a clear commercial need.
- It loads properly on desktop and mobile devices.
- It contains enough original information to justify a citation.
- Its title and headings match the subject described in the campaign brief.
- It provides a logical next action for visitors.

Anchor text also needs restraint. Use a mixture of branded, URL-based, descriptive, and naturally phrased anchors. If the page is about accounting software for small businesses, examples might include “the platform,” “small business accounting software,” the company name, or a plain URL. Avoid forcing the exact same commercial phrase into every placement.

The purpose of an anchor is to help a reader understand where the link goes. It is not a command to repeat a keyword as often as possible. A varied, contextually appropriate approach is easier to defend and usually produces better editorial results.

## Step 3: Turn the campaign into a publisher-ready brief

A vague request such as “get five strong links about marketing” leaves too many decisions unresolved. A useful brief should be short enough to understand quickly but specific enough to guide the placement.

Include:

- The target URL or URLs.
- A concise description of the business or resource.
- Topics that are relevant.
- Topics, claims, or industries to avoid.
- Preferred language and market.
- Suggested anchor variations.
- The number of placements requested.
- Any requirements for page visibility or indexing.
- The desired tone, such as educational, practical, or editorial.

Here is a simple example:

> Promote a guide to reducing energy use in small offices. Reach office managers and operations teams in the United States. Relevant subjects include commercial lighting, energy audits, workplace efficiency, and utility costs. Avoid unsupported savings claims and overly promotional copy. Use natural branded or descriptive anchors. Request six public placements on relevant pages.

This format gives the publisher a clear editorial direction without attempting to script every sentence. If a campaign requires legal or compliance review, identify the restricted claims in advance. It is easier to prevent a problem in the brief than to correct it after publication.

## Step 4: Select a manageable quantity and pace

More placements are not automatically better. Start with a quantity that you can inspect and report on properly. A small pilot can reveal whether the topics, page quality, and writing style match your standards before you expand the campaign.

A practical starting sequence might look like this:

1. Request three to five placements for a new target page.
2. Review the live results carefully.
3. Identify which topics and formats produced the most relevant pages.
4. Adjust the brief if necessary.
5. Request the next batch only after the first batch meets expectations.

This staged approach is useful for agencies managing several clients because it reduces the risk of spending the full budget before quality has been validated. It also creates a clear record of what changed between campaigns.

## Step 5: Review every public placement

A placement is not finished when a URL appears in a delivery list. Open the page in a normal browser and inspect it as a visitor would.

Use this checklist:

- Is the page publicly accessible without a login?
- Does the URL resolve correctly?
- Is the target link present and clickable?
- Does the surrounding text make sense?
- Is the page related to the target subject?
- Does the article look like a real piece of content rather than a page filled with unrelated links?
- Is the publisher site functioning normally?
- Is the placement visible in the page source or rendered content as appropriate?
- Are there obvious signs of copied, spun, or nonsensical writing?
- Does the page create a reasonable experience for a potential visitor?

Record the live URL, publication date, target URL, anchor text, topical relationship, and review status. If the client needs evidence, provide the public page rather than a screenshot of an internal dashboard. The live placement is the deliverable.

When a placement does not meet the brief, document the issue clearly. A missing link, irrelevant article, broken page, or misleading claim should be flagged for correction or replacement. A consistent review policy protects both the agency and the client.

## Step 6: Measure useful outcomes instead of vanity totals

Track more than the number of links acquired. A basic campaign report can include:

- Number of requested placements.
- Number of live placements.
- Percentage accepted after review.
- Relevance rating for each page.
- Anchor text distribution.
- Target pages supported.
- Referral visits, where measurable.
- Search visibility changes over an appropriate period.
- Notes about replacement requests or editorial issues.

Search performance rarely changes in a perfectly linear way, and no ethical workflow can guarantee a particular ranking position. Rankings are influenced by content quality, technical accessibility, competition, internal linking, user intent, and many other factors.

Use the report to answer practical questions. Did the placements reach the intended audience? Did they support pages that matter commercially? Were the articles relevant enough to keep? Did visitors arrive through the links? Did the campaign reveal topics that deserve better content?

Agencies can also use these records to improve client communication. Instead of saying that work was “submitted,” the report can show which public pages went live, why they were relevant, and how the campaign will be refined next.

## Step 7: Build a repeatable operating routine

Once the first campaign has been reviewed, turn the process into a simple routine. Create a brief template with fields for audience, target page, subject, exclusions, anchor guidance, quantity, and review requirements. Keep a separate campaign log so each client’s goals and placements remain distinct.

A weekly routine might include:

- Monday: select target pages and prepare briefs.
- Tuesday: submit requests and check campaign status.
- Wednesday: review newly delivered pages.
- Thursday: request corrections or replacements.
- Friday: update the report and record lessons for the next brief.

This schedule is only an example. The important principle is ownership at each stage. Someone should be responsible for defining the campaign, checking the live results, and communicating the outcome.

For agencies, document approval rules before work begins. Decide who can approve a placement, which claims require client review, and how long a publisher has to correct an issue. Clear policies prevent rushed decisions when several campaigns finish at once.

## Common mistakes to avoid

The most frequent mistake is treating a placement count as the entire campaign. Ten irrelevant pages are not necessarily more valuable than three highly relevant public pages. Another mistake is writing briefs that emphasize keywords while ignoring the reader, publisher context, and target page.

Avoid promising guaranteed rankings, instant authority, or permanent traffic. Be transparent about what the campaign can deliver: public placements, relevant editorial context, referral opportunities, and support for a broader SEO strategy.

It is also risky to skip the final review. A link that exists but points to the wrong page, appears beside unrelated content, or sits on a broken publisher site should not be reported as a successful placement.

Finally, do not let the tool dictate the strategy. Begin with the client’s audience and business objectives, then use the publishing workflow to support those goals.
